Final Course Project: AI, Economic Perceptions, and Consumer Sentiment Ahead of
the 2026 Midterm Elections
Course: AI Applications in Finance
Duration: Six days
Deliverables: Research report, reproducible analysis, monitoring dashboard, and
summary Power Point presentation
Project objective
Develop and validate an AI-assisted framework to measure economic narratives in news
and social media and investigate whether they help explain differences between US
consumer sentiment and measured economic conditions ahead of the 2026 midterm
elections.
Your central task is to distinguish among three possible explanations:
    • Consumers are responding to economic pressures inadequately captured by
       headline indicators.
    • Consumers’ perceptions reﬂect selective information, framing, or cognitive biases.
    • The apparent discrepancy reﬂects measurement differences, timing, or an
       incomplete statistical model.
Do not assume that pessimism is irrational, that negative coverage is biased, or that
media exposure causes declining sentiment. These are hypotheses to test.
Required research questions
Your project must address:
    1. How can we measure bias in news coverage or social media?
    2. How can we determine the impact that such bias may be having on depressing
       US consumer sentiment, as measured by the University of Michigan survey,
       ahead of the upcoming midterm elections?
    3. How much of the apparent sentiment discrepancy can unemployment, inﬂation,
       and real GDP growth explain?
    4. Which candidate perceptual mechanisms—such as negativity bias, availability bias,
       conﬁrmation bias, or anchoring to past prices—can your data actually measure?
    5. Does media analysis improve predictions of consumer sentiment beyond economic
       indicators alone?
    6. What evidence would contradict a media-bias explanation?

2. Build a balanced media corpus
Collect economic coverage from a documented selection of national and local news
sources and at least one social-media platform. If historical platform access is limited,
narrow your claims accordingly.
Document:
    • Search terms, dates, source selection, and inclusion criteria.
    • Whether observations represent headlines, full articles, posts, or comments.
    • Geographic relevance and available audience information.
    • Duplicate stories, reposts, advertisements, and suspected automated activity.
    • Missing data, access restrictions, and sampling limitations.
Distinguish content supply, engagement, and actual exposure. Likes and reposts are
imperfect attention measures; they do not establish what survey respondents
encountered.

Human-label a stratiﬁed sample of at least 300 items using two independent annotators.

Text can establish framing or selective coverage; it cannot, by itself, establish a
reader’s cognitive bias. Claims about conﬁrmation or availability bias require
corresponding exposure, recall, belief, or experimental evidence.
4. Evaluate association and causal impact separately
Compare three models:
    1. Economic indicators only.
    2. Economic indicators plus media measures.
    3. Economic indicators, media measures, and additional household or political
        controls.
Use chronological holdouts or rolling forecasts. Report predictive accuracy, coefficient
stability, uncertainty, and sensitivity to alternative speciﬁcations. Account for
autocorrelation, seasonality, lag selection, and multiple testing.
For causal analysis, choose one feasible design:
    • Randomized framing experiment: Assign consenting participants balanced factual
        summaries or alternative truthful framings of the same economic information.
        Measure changes in beliefs and sentiment-related responses.
    • Quasi-experiment: Exploit plausibly external differences in media exposure,
        explaining treatment assignment, assumptions, spillovers, and pre-trends.
    • Observational analysis with a causal-design proposal: Estimate associations and
        provide a detailed plan for stronger identiﬁcation if suitable exposure data are
        unavailable.
Address reverse causality: pessimistic consumers may seek negative content, and
publishers may respond to audience demand. Economic shocks may also affect both
coverage and sentiment.
An experimental effect on your sample does not establish an effect on the national
Michigan index. Explain what exposure estimates, representative sampling, weighting, and
transportability assumptions would be needed to make that connection.

    • Source composition, missingness, and model-performance indicators.
